## What Is Plagiarism?

A type of academic dishonesty:

- Copying someone else's ideas, words, or sentences without giving due credit — e.g. a peer, web page, journal article or scholarly source, Wikipedia, or something composed by AI
- Allowing someone else to copy from your work without them giving due credit to you

**Unintentional plagiarism is also considered plagiarism.** Pleading ignorance is not an excuse — it is your responsibility to educate yourself about plagiarism and its many forms.

## Two Forms of Plagiarism

### Intentional Plagiarism

- Copying from a source with no quotation marks and/or citation so the reader thinks it's your own work
- Copying a peer's paper or using AI to write your papers
- Using pictures, graphics, algorithms, or code without citation (these should be treated like any other quoted information)

### Unintentional Plagiarism

- Paraphrases that are too close to the original words and sentence structure of the author (does not matter if there is a citation)
- Too much direct quoting / not enough of your own original content
- Not including both a citation in the body **and** in the reference list at the end of your paper

## Plagiarism and Paraphrasing: What's the Connection?

**Paraphrasing:** rephrasing others' ideas/words in your own words, along with giving credit. Usually also includes adding your own analysis or combining it with other sources.

> [!warning] Sloppy or careless paraphrasing leads to (unintentional) plagiarism. No matter the intent, plagiarism will be penalized.

## Why Do We Use Sources and Cite Them?

- Builds credibility and authority as a writer; shows you've done background research and learned about the topic
- Connects your writing to bigger topics in the field — research happens in a community where we all need and depend on each other
- Allows readers to find reliable sources

## What Do We Mean by "Citing"? (3 Ways to Give Credit)

1. **Quoting the author's words** — using the exact string of words from the author. Use quotation marks `"…"` around the quote with an in-text citation. The words within the quotes must be identical to the original; do not leave out words if they change the author's original meaning.
2. **Paraphrase the author's words** — restate the author's words using synonyms and a changed sentence structure. You should be able to explain the idea to someone in conversation (you've internalized the content). Must include an in-text citation.
3. **Summarize the passage** — similar to paraphrasing but gives only the main points; shorter than a paraphrase. Used for a short description of the original without adding your own opinion. Must include an in-text citation.

## No Need to Cite When...

- You are using **"common knowledge"** — facts commonly known and appearing in several sources, e.g.:
    - George Washington was the first president of the USA.
    - Unethical business practices led to the Watergate scandal.
    - The Internet of Things refers to the interconnection through the Internet of computing devices found in everyday objects, enabling them to send and receive data.
- You are using information from your own research, experiments, or observations — **but not** if it has been published.

> [!tip] If engineers are your readers, you may be able to discuss well-known facts without citing — but if it's the general public, "common knowledge" to you may not be to them. **When in doubt — cite!**

## In-Text and End-of-Text Citations

Two places where you cite sources:

1. **In-text citations** — within the body of your paper, usually at the end of sentences, quotations, paragraphs, etc.
2. **End-of-text citations** — a list of references at the end of the paper, usually titled _References_.

## APA vs. IEEE Referencing Styles

APA (American Psychological Association) and IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) are common reference styles in STEM fields. APA is used in social and natural sciences; **most computer engineering journals use IEEE style.**

||APA|IEEE|
|---|---|---|
|In-text citation|Author + date — e.g. _"According to Tanenbaum (1997) …"_|Numbers in square brackets — e.g. `[1]`|
|Reference list order|Alphabetical|Order of appearance in text|
|Page format|Double-spaced, paragraph format|Double-column format|

🔗 [IEEE Style Reference Manual (PDF)](https://www.ieee.org/content/dam/ieee-org/ieee/web/org/conferences/style_references_manual.pdf)

## Turnitin & AI Detection Scores — What's Acceptable?

- All work submitted on Canvas goes through **Turnitin**
- A Turnitin score of **20% or lower** is acceptable
- AI written/composed work is also detected — if over **50%**, it might lead to a grade penalty

> [!caution] A Turnitin score of 15% or lower does **not** indicate anything about AI use. A student can have 100% AI-written work, but the Turnitin score could be as low as 5%.

## Summary

- It is never okay to use material from a source without citations. Always include an in-text citation for any idea you use from a source; for any words/word strings you incorporate, use `"…"` with an in-text citation.
- Each reference in your reference list should also be included within the body of your paper as an in-text citation.
- Understand the many forms of plagiarism.
- Give yourself enough time to complete an assignment.
- Do not collaborate on assignments unless explicitly stated — because it has serious consequences!
